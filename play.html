Mirror Magic

Symmetry10 min

"I see symmetry everywhere: in spider webs, in leaves, even in your face! Let's find the line where two halves match."

Video coming soon Spider shows the fold-and-paint trick · about 60 sec

The big idea

  1. A line of symmetry cuts a picture into two matching halves.If you folded the picture on that line, the halves would land right on top of each other.
  2. One half is the mirror image of the other.Everything gets flipped. A spot on the far left has a twin on the far right.
  3. Count from the line.If a square is 2 steps to the left of the line, its twin is 2 steps to the right. Same row, same color.
Make it with art

Fold a paper in half and open it. Paint blobs on one side only, fold it shut, press, and peel it open. You just made a symmetric butterfly.

For grown-ups: lines of symmetry (Common Core 4.G.A.3), with an early start for grades 1–3.

Game: Finish the picture

The left half is done. Paint the right half so it mirrors the left. Pick a color, then tap squares.

Stop 2 · Hosted by the Environmental Engineer

Honeycomb Hunt

Shapes & anglesEnvironmental engineering15 min

"Bees are nature's engineers! They build with hexagons so no space and no wax gets wasted. How could we build like bees?"

Video coming soon Bee traces a honeycomb · about 60 sec

The big idea

  1. A tessellation is a pattern of shapes with no gaps and no overlaps.Floor tiles, brick walls and honeycombs are all tessellations.
  2. Look where the corners meet.All the way around one point is a full turn, which is 360 degrees (360°).
  3. The corners have to add up to exactly 360°.A hexagon corner is 120°. Three of them make 120 + 120 + 120 = 360. A perfect fit!
  4. Bees pick the shape that wastes the least.Honeycomb walls are made of wax, and bees must eat lots of honey to make wax. Hexagons hold the same honey as triangles or squares but need less wall, so the hive saves its food.
  5. Engineers ask the bee's question."How do we build it with less material and less waste?" That's why airplanes, doors and even cardboard use honeycomb shapes inside. They stay strong and light while using less stuff.
Make it with art

Reuse an empty cereal box: cut out a hexagon and use it as a stencil. Trace it, slide it so one edge touches, and trace again. Keep going until the page is a honeycomb, then color it in. Bonus: count how many cells you made from one stencil and zero new cardboard.

For grown-ups: angles as parts of a 360° turn (Common Core 4.MD.C.5), adding angles (4.MD.C.7), and comparing design solutions (NGSS 3-5-ETS1-2).

Game: Which shapes tile?

Pick a shape. Add tiles around the dot and watch the corners add up. Then decide: does it tile with no gaps?
