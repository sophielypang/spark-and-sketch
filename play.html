Stop 1 · Hosted by the Mathematician

Mirror Magic

Symmetry10 min

"I see symmetry everywhere: in spider webs, in leaves, even in your face! Let's find the line where two halves match."

Video coming soon Spider shows the fold-and-paint trick · about 60 sec

The big idea

  1. A line of symmetry cuts a picture into two matching halves.If you folded the picture on that line, the halves would land right on top of each other.
  2. One half is the mirror image of the other.Everything gets flipped. A spot on the far left has a twin on the far right.
  3. Count from the line.If a square is 2 steps to the left of the line, its twin is 2 steps to the right. Same row, same color.
Make it with art

Fold a paper in half and open it. Paint blobs on one side only, fold it shut, press, and peel it open. You just made a symmetric butterfly.

For grown-ups: lines of symmetry (Common Core 4.G.A.3), with an early start for grades 1–3.

Game: Finish the picture

The left half is done. Paint the right half so it mirrors the left. Pick a color, then tap squares.

Stop 2 · Hosted by the Environmental Engineer

Honeycomb Hunt

Shapes & anglesEnvironmental engineering15 min

"Bees are nature's engineers! They build with hexagons so no space and no wax gets wasted. How could we build like bees?"

Video coming soon Bee traces a honeycomb · about 60 sec

The big idea

  1. A tessellation is a pattern of shapes with no gaps and no overlaps.Floor tiles, brick walls and honeycombs are all tessellations.
  2. Look where the corners meet.All the way around one point is a full turn, which is 360 degrees (360°).
  3. The corners have to add up to exactly 360°.A hexagon corner is 120°. Three of them make 120 + 120 + 120 = 360. A perfect fit!
  4. Bees pick the shape that wastes the least.Honeycomb walls are made of wax, and bees must eat lots of honey to make wax. Hexagons hold the same honey as triangles or squares but need less wall, so the hive saves its food.
  5. Engineers ask the bee's question."How do we build it with less material and less waste?" That's why airplanes, doors and even cardboard use honeycomb shapes inside. They stay strong and light while using less stuff.
Make it with art

Reuse an empty cereal box: cut out a hexagon and use it as a stencil. Trace it, slide it so one edge touches, and trace again. Keep going until the page is a honeycomb, then color it in. Bonus: count how many cells you made from one stencil and zero new cardboard.

For grown-ups: angles as parts of a 360° turn (Common Core 4.MD.C.5), adding angles (4.MD.C.7), and comparing design solutions (NGSS 3-5-ETS1-2).

Game: Which shapes tile?

Pick a shape. Add tiles around the dot and watch the corners add up. Then decide: does it tile with no gaps?

Stop 3 · Hosted by the Architect

The Trinket Box

3D shapesArchitecture15 min

"Every building starts flat on paper. Let's find the flat shapes that fold up into a box!"

Video coming soon Beaver folds a cereal box into a trinket box · about 60 sec

The big idea

  1. A net is a flat pattern that folds into a 3D shape.A box (a cube) has 6 square faces, so its net is made of 6 squares joined edge to edge.
  2. Not every 6-square shape works.Some fold so two squares land on the same side and another side stays open.
  3. Picture the floor.Choose one square as the bottom. The squares touching it fold up to be walls. Each wall needs its own spot, and one square has to land on top.
  4. Architects think flat and tall at the same time.A floor plan is flat. The building is tall. Nets are how you move between the two.
Make it with art

Draw a cross of six squares on a flattened cereal box (each square about as wide as your hand). Cut it out, fold along every line, and tape the edges. Decorate the outside before you fold: that's the easy way. Now you have a trinket box.

For grown-ups: nets of three-dimensional shapes (Common Core 6.G.A.4, started early) and spatial reasoning.

Game: Fold or flop?

Look at the flat shape. Will it fold into a closed box with six sides? Decide, then see the answer.

Stop 4 · Hosted by the Coder

Robot Painter

CodingStep by step15 min

"A program is just a list of steps. My robot does exactly what I say, so I have to say it exactly right."

Video coming soon Python programs a friend to draw a house · about 60 sec

The big idea

  1. A program is a list of instructions, in order.A computer does exactly what the list says. Nothing more, nothing less.
  2. Big jobs are made of tiny steps."Paint a line" is really "move, paint, move, paint, move, paint."
  3. When it goes wrong, that's a bug.Find the one step that's wrong and fix it. Coders do this all day, and it's not a mistake, it's the job.
Make it with art

Be the programmer: write 8 instructions for drawing a house ("draw a square", "draw a triangle on top"...). Hand them to a friend who has to follow them exactly, with no guessing. Compare the drawing to the house in your head. Where was the bug?

For grown-ups: sequences and debugging (CSTA K-12 standards 1A-AP-10 and 1A-AP-14).

Game: Program the painter

Paint every outlined square. Add steps to the program, then press Run and watch the robot follow it.

Stop 5 · Hosted by the Physicist

Ramp Racer

EnergyFriction15 min

"Why does a ball roll farther off a tall ramp? And why does carpet stop it so fast? Let's test it and find out!"

Video coming soon Dolphin races marbles down a cardboard ramp · about 60 sec

The big idea

  1. High up means stored energy.Lift a ball to the top of a ramp and it has energy waiting to be used.
  2. Rolling down turns stored energy into moving energy.A taller ramp gives the ball more energy, so it's going faster at the bottom.
  3. Friction slows things down.The floor rubs against the ball. Rough carpet rubs a lot, so the ball stops soon. Smooth ice barely rubs at all.
  4. Physicists change one thing at a time.Change only the height, or only the floor, and you can tell which one made the difference.
Make it with art

Make a ramp from a cereal box and books. Roll a marble onto paper, then a towel, then a tile floor. Put a sticker where it stops each time. Draw the three stickers as a bar chart with your own colors.

For grown-ups: energy and speed (NGSS 4-PS3-1), forces and motion (3-PS2-1), and fair tests.

Game: Land in the zone

Pick a ramp height and a floor so the ball stops inside the green zone. Then press Roll.

Stop 6 · Hosted by the Aerospace Engineer

Paper Plane Lab

FlightTesting20 min

"I toss everything to see if it flies. Then I ask why. Today we're tossing paper planes!"

Video coming soon Falcon tests three planes down a hallway · about 60 sec

The big idea

  1. Wings push air down, and the air pushes the wing up.That upward push is called lift. No air moving over the wing, no lift.
  2. The angle matters.Tip the nose up a little and the wing gets more lift. Tip it up too much and the air can't flow smoothly. The plane stalls and drops.
  3. Weight up front keeps the nose steady.A paper clip on the nose stops the plane from flipping up into a stall.
  4. Change one thing, then throw again.That's how engineers find out what really matters.
Make it with art

Fold a paper plane and decorate the wings. Throw it 5 times with no clip and 5 times with a paper clip on the nose. Count your footsteps to where it lands and draw the two sets of throws as dots on a chart.

For grown-ups: forces on objects (NGSS 3-PS2-1) and testing designs (3-5-ETS1-3).

Game: Fly through the hoop

Set up the plane so it flies through the hoop. Change one thing at a time and watch what happens.

Stop 7 · Hosted by the Mechanical Engineer

Gear Garage

MachinesRatios15 min

"Gears are my favorite. Turn one and the whole machine wakes up. Let's build a gear train!"

Video coming soon Octopus builds cardboard gears · about 60 sec

The big idea

  1. Gears are wheels with teeth.When the teeth mesh, turning one gear turns the next one.
  2. Meshed gears turn in opposite directions.Add a third gear and the last one turns the same way as the first again.
  3. Size changes speed.A small gear turning a big gear makes it turn slower, but with a stronger push. Big turning small: faster, but weaker.
  4. Count teeth to predict it.A 12-tooth gear turning a 24-tooth gear: 12 ÷ 24 = half the speed. Every turn of the small gear is half a turn of the big one.
Make it with art

Cut three gears out of cardboard: trace a bottle cap, a mug, and a bowl, then cut teeth around the edges. Pin them to a cardboard box with pushpins so the teeth touch. Draw a face on one and turn another. Which way does the face spin?

For grown-ups: simple machines, ratios as a first look (Common Core 6.RP.A.1), and engineering design (NGSS 3-5-ETS1-1).
